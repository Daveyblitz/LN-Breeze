## Bitcoin and the speed problem

Bitcoin's base layer was designed for security and decentralisation, not speed. A transaction submitted to the Bitcoin network takes roughly 10 minutes to confirm in a block, and during periods of high demand, fees can spike unpredictably. For everyday payments — buying a coffee, paying for a subscription, tipping a creator — this is impractical.

The Lightning Network is Bitcoin's answer to this. It is a second layer that enables near-instant payments between parties without touching the base blockchain on every transaction.

## How Lightning works (the short version)

Two parties open a **payment channel** by locking some Bitcoin in a shared on-chain transaction. After that, they can send payments back and forth between themselves — or through a network of other channels — without touching the blockchain. Each payment updates a shared balance sheet. When they are done, they close the channel and settle the final balance on-chain.

From a developer's perspective, you do not need to understand the channel mechanics to build on Lightning. What matters is the invoice model.

## BOLT11 invoices

When you want to receive a Lightning payment, you generate a **BOLT11 invoice**. BOLT11 is the standard format for Lightning payment requests — every Lightning wallet and node speaks it.

A BOLT11 invoice is a long encoded string that always starts with `lnbc` on mainnet:

```
lnbc10u1pn2k3j4pp5a3f9bc2d1e4f5a6b7c8d9e0f...
```

Inside that string, encoded, are:

- The amount to pay (in millisatoshis)
- A description or description hash
- An expiry time
- The destination node's public key
- A payment hash
- A cryptographic signature

The payer's wallet decodes this, finds a route through the Lightning Network, and sends the payment. You do not need to decode it yourself — just generate it and show it as a QR code.

## Satoshis and millisatoshis

Bitcoin is divisible down to 8 decimal places. The smallest unit on the base layer is a **satoshi** (1 sat = 0.00000001 BTC).

Lightning goes one step further and uses **millisatoshis** (msats) internally — 1 satoshi = 1,000 millisatoshis. This allows sub-satoshi precision in routing fees, though invoices themselves are typically denominated in whole satoshis.

When the LN-Breeze wallet endpoint returns a balance, it returns millisatoshis. Divide by 1,000 to get satoshis.

```
2,500,000 msats ÷ 1,000 = 2,500 sats
```

## Payment hashes

Every Lightning invoice has a **payment hash** — a 64-character hex string that uniquely identifies it. It is derived from a secret (the payment preimage) that only the recipient knows.

When a payment arrives, the recipient reveals the preimage. The network verifies it matches the hash. This is what makes Lightning payments trustless — the proof of payment is cryptographic.

In LN-Breeze, the payment hash is how you track a specific invoice:
- You create an invoice → receive a `paymentHash`
- You poll `GET /api/v1/invoices/:hash` using that hash
- When `paid: true` comes back, the payment has been cryptographically settled

## Invoice expiry

Every BOLT11 invoice has an expiry time. LN-Breeze sets this to **10 minutes** from creation. After expiry, the invoice cannot be paid — any attempt will be rejected by the Lightning Network.

If a customer does not pay within the window, generate a new invoice. There is no way to extend a Lightning invoice's expiry after it has been created.

## Why this matters for your integration

Three things to keep in mind when building:

1. **Generate invoices on demand** — do not pre-generate and cache them. Generate one when your customer is ready to pay.
2. **Store the payment hash** — it is your only link between an invoice and a payment confirmation.
3. **Handle expiry** — always track `expiresAt` and show the customer a clear message if the invoice expires before they pay. Let them generate a fresh one.
